There is a right amount for a game-playing AI to know, and it turns out to be
exactly what a person sitting across the table knows. Not less, which is the
mistake everyone expects. And not more, which is the mistake I did not.

I found the boundary by overshooting it in both directions. An agent that knew
less than a player would was worth about fifteen points less than one that knew
what a player knows. An agent that knew *more* than any player could, because I
handed it the true hidden cards, was worth five and a half points less. The human
boundary is not a limitation to be tolerated. It is the optimum, and it was
measured from both sides.

The last post ended at parity with a few hundred lines of if-statements, with the
network finally naming its moves properly. This post is about the other half of
the interface: what the network is given to think about, and why the answer is a
line rather than a direction.

<Concept title="Who sees what: the boundary, in code">

A hidden-information game is two private views laid over one shared one. Every
zone declares who may see its contents: **public** (the market, the discard pile),
**owner** (your hand: you see it, others do not), or **hidden** (the deck: nobody
sees it, including its owner). When the engine builds an observation for a seat, a
projection walks every zone and hands that seat what it is entitled to: full
contents, or the count alone.

The distinction that matters is that "count only" is not "nothing." An opponent's
hand hides its identities, but its count is public in essentially every card game
ever printed, and so are their camels, their tokens, and everything in the middle
of the table.

## Knowing less than a player

The first agents in this series knew less than a player in a way that is easy to
miss, because it looked like a reasonable engineering shortcut. The encoder
reported each zone as a count of cards. The market holds five cards. Your hand
holds four. That is enough to play, in the sense that the agent beat a random
opponent every time, and it is much less than a person knows, because a person
knows *which* five cards.

I had a growing suspicion that this kind of gap, not the network's size or its
training, was where the missing strength lived. The suspicion is testable with the
experiment design I trust most: isolate one lever at a time and pull. Lever one,
capacity: the same agent with a network four times larger. Nothing. Lever two,
optimization: the same agent trained considerably longer, against a league of its
own past versions. Nothing. Lever three, information: the same network, the same
training, and an encoder that reports the *composition* of every zone the seat is
entitled to see. Not "the market holds five cards" but "the market holds two
cloth, a diamond, a spice, and a camel."

The new agent beat the old champion in 60.3% of 1,200 seat-balanced games,
confidence interval 57.5 to 63.1. Two nulls and a hit.

Only the third arm changed what the agent could see." />

That closed most of the gap between the agent and a player. Not all of it. There
is a kind of knowledge that is not visible *anywhere* on the table right now, and
is still legitimately yours, because you watched it happen.

Play Jaipur against a person and take the diamond from the market. Your hand is
hidden, but your opponent knows the diamond is in it. They saw you take it. Nobody
calls this cheating; it is called paying attention, and every competent card player
does it. The composition encoder could not do it. The moment a card crossed into a
hidden zone, its identity was gone from the observation, because identity was
stored on the zone, and the zone says hidden.

<Concept title="Perfect recall: knowledge belongs to the history, not the zone">

The engine keeps a running set of card identities that are public knowledge. A card
entering a public zone joins the set. A card taken from public view into a player's
hand *stays* in the set, because everyone watched where it went. A card that enters
the deck, or gets caught in a shuffle, leaves the set: after that, no honest
observer could still track it.

The observation then reports an opponent's hand as what it honestly is to you: the
cards you watched arrive, plus a count of the rest. Not "five unknown cards" but
"the diamond, and four unknown cards."

Trained head to head against a control with the identical recipe and only the
encoder changed, the perfect-recall agent won 55.0% of games, interval 51.9 to
58.1. Against the reigning champion, 59.3%. The win came with a visible change in
style: the new agent takes more goods than its predecessor, which is what racing
looks like. It is not just scoring its own hand anymore. It can see what the
opponent is collecting, and it has started denying.

Ten points for seeing what is on the table. Five more for remembering what
crossed it. The agent now knew what a player knows, and every step toward that
line had paid.

## Knowing more than a player

Which is why the next experiment was so hard to accept.

The strongest agent in the stable by then wrapped the network in search, and the
search dealt with hidden cards the only way it could: by guessing. Before
thinking, it filled in the opponent's hand and the deck with a random arrangement
consistent with everything public, thought in that imagined world, then repeated
with fresh guesses and averaged. Each guess is called a determinization, and the
averaging is what keeps the search honest about what it does not know.

In a self-play harness I control both seats, which suggested an obvious shortcut.
Skip the guessing. Pin the *true* opponent hand into every imagined world. No
training, no new machinery: just stop lying to the search about the one zone that
matters most. Information had just proven to be the only lever that moved. This
was the purest information I could give it.

The genuinely blind searcher won 52.1% of its games against the heuristic. Handed
the true opponent hand, the same searcher won 46.5%. Handed the true deck order
instead, 48.9%. Every flavor of truth made it worse, and the opponent's hand, the
most valuable-looking secret at the table, made it worst of all.

The mechanism took a while to believe. The strength of a determinizing search
*lives in the variety of its guesses*. Averaging over many imagined worlds forces
the agent toward moves that are good against the whole cloud of hands the opponent
might hold, which is another way of saying it hedges. Pin one world as the truth
and the hedge collapses: the search happily commits to lines that exploit that
exact hand, played by a network whose every judgment was formed under uncertainty.
Handed certainty, it is off the edge of its own experience, confidently wrong in a
direction it was never trained to recognize. The averaging was not a workaround
for missing information. It was load-bearing.

There is a second reading, and I think it is the more important one. The network
was trained on observations drawn from the player's side of the boundary. Every
weight in it encodes what a *player* should do with what a *player* knows. Feed
it knowledge from past the boundary and there is no part of it that knows what to
do with that, in the same way that a person handed their opponent's hand mid-game
would not suddenly play optimally either. They would play a game they had never
practiced.

## Drawing the line

So the boundary is the optimum, and the engine's job is to draw it precisely. Most
of that is the visibility contract from the first block. The part that took actual
care is the forgetting rule, because it is where "what a player knows" gets
decided in the ambiguous cases.

The rule is deliberately conservative in one direction. Any passage through a
hidden pile or a shuffle erases a card from the record, even in corner cases where
a sharp player might still infer something. Forgetting a still-knowable card
merely plays a little cautiously, a point or two short of the line. Remembering a
card that is no longer knowable crosses it, and the experiment above is what
crossing it costs. The test suite pins the invariant exactly: the set of cards the
observation claims to know in a hidden hand equals the recorded public knowledge
intersected with that hand, no more, no less.

The night the pinned-hand result came in, the note in the journal read: value
must be trained in, not pinned. Knowledge helps an agent when it is part of what
the agent learned from, and hurts when it is injected into a system built for its
absence. I suspect that is not specific to card games. Anyone who has bolted an
oracle onto a learned system, and watched it get worse, has run this experiment.

So here is where things stand. The network sees every number a player at the
table sees, remembers what it watched, forgets what it could not honestly know,
and names its moves properly. It races, it denies, it is measurably better than
every version before it. And against a few hundred lines of if-statements, the
ladder still reads level. Knowing was worth points, in exactly one direction, up
to exactly one line. Whatever is left is not about knowing.
