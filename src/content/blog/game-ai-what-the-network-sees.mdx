The last post ended at parity. The pointer head fixed how the network answers, the
ladder read 49.5% against the heuristic, and the obvious question was where the next
points were hiding. I had two suspects left: what the network computes, and what the
network is given to compute with. The first suspect had already been cleared once, so
this post is about the second, and about the strangest experimental result this
project has produced so far.

It starts with an audit.

## An overnight audit, and an embarrassing finding

Before launching another expensive training run, I pointed a swarm of review agents
at the whole pipeline: the environment wrapper, the reward plumbing, the encoder,
the works. Fifteen confirmed findings came back. Three of them gated everything, and
one of those three is the subject of this post.

The network was blind to its opponent.

Not to the opponent's secrets. To the opponent's *public* state. In Jaipur, your
rival's hand size, their herd of camels, and the tokens they have banked are all
open information: they are sitting on the table, and any human player glances at
them constantly. The encoder reported every one of those numbers as the hidden
sentinel, negative one. The network was playing a hidden-information game with more
hidden from it than the rules hide.

<Concept title="Who sees what: visibility as an engine contract">

Every zone in a game declares who may see its contents: **public** (the market, the
discard pile), **owner** (your hand: you see it, others do not), or **hidden** (the
deck: nobody sees it, including its owner). When the engine builds an observation
for a seat, a projection walks every zone and decides what that seat gets: full
contents, count only, or nothing.

The subtle part is that "count only" and "nothing" are different answers. An
opponent's hand hides its *identities*, but its *count* is public in essentially
every card game ever printed. The bug was answering "nothing" where the rules say
"count only."

<BlindfoldDemo />

</Concept>

The bitter comedy is that the heuristic the network keeps losing to does not look
at the opponent either. Those few hundred lines of if-statements sell the moment a
set forms and never once consider the other player. An agent that could see the
opponent could, in principle, race it: hold goods it is about to want, time sales
against its tempo. That was the clearest path to actually beating it, and no
trainable agent could take that path, because the encoder was feeding every method
the same blindfold.

The fix took twenty minutes. The observation did not even change width; the slots
existed and held the sentinel. Twenty minutes, against weeks of architecture work.
Keep that ratio in mind, because this post repeats it.

## Three levers, one variable each

The blindfold was not the first hint of this pattern. It was the newest instance of
a suspicion that had been building for weeks: maybe the agent was never short on
brainpower. Maybe it was short on information. The suspicion had already been put
to a clean test once, with the experiment design I trust most: isolate one lever at
a time and pull.

Lever one, capacity: train the same agent with a network four times larger. Result:
nothing. Lever two, optimization: train the same agent considerably longer, against
a league of past versions. Result: nothing. Lever three, information: keep the
network and the training identical, and change only what the agent sees. Instead of
reporting each zone as a count of cards, the encoder now reports the *composition*
of every zone it is entitled to see: not "the market holds five cards" but "the
market holds two cloth, a diamond, a spice, and a camel."

Result: the new agent beat the old champion in 60.3% of 1,200 seat-balanced games,
confidence interval 57.5 to 63.1. Two nulls and a hit.

<ZoomImage src="/images/posts/ml4-levers.png" alt="Bar chart of three experiments: a bigger network and more training both land on the 50% line (null), while the richer v2 observation wins 60.3% with a confidence interval of 57.5 to 63.1." caption="Same network, same training recipe in every arm. Only the third arm changed what the agent could see." />

I want to be precise about what surprised me here, because "more information helps"
sounds obvious. What surprised me is what *failed* to help. A bigger brain is the
default reflex of this entire field, and it did nothing. Watching the table more
carefully was worth ten points. The ceiling was not the network's capacity to think.
It was the quality of what it was given to think about.

## Remembering what you watched

The composition encoder sees everything it is entitled to see at this instant. The
next rung on the ladder is subtler: some knowledge is not visible *anywhere* on the
table right now, and is still legitimately yours, because you watched it happen.

Play Jaipur against a person and take the diamond from the market. Your hand is
hidden, but your opponent knows the diamond is in it. They saw you take it. Nobody
calls this cheating; it is called paying attention, and every competent card player
does it. The count encoder cannot do it. The moment a card crosses into a hidden
zone, its identity is gone from the observation, because identity was stored on the
zone, and the zone says hidden.

<Concept title="Perfect recall: knowledge belongs to the history, not the zone">

The engine keeps a running set of card identities that are public knowledge. A card
entering a public zone joins the set. A card taken from public view into a player's
hand *stays* in the set, because everyone watched where it went. A card that enters
the deck, or gets caught in a shuffle, leaves the set: after that, no honest
observer could still track it.

The observation then reports an opponent's hand as what it honestly is to you: the
cards you watched arrive, plus a count of the rest. Not "five unknown cards" but
"the diamond, and four unknown cards."

<RecallDemo />

</Concept>

The design question that took actual care is when to *forget*. The engine's rule is
deliberately conservative in one direction: any passage through a hidden pile or a
shuffle erases a card from the record, even in corner cases where a sharp player
might still infer something. Forgetting a still-knowable card merely plays a little
cautiously. The reverse mistake, remembering a card that is no longer knowable, is
an information leak, and leaks are the one thing an honest game engine cannot have.
The test suite pins the exact invariant: the set of cards the observation claims to
know in a hidden hand equals the recorded public knowledge intersected with that
hand, no more, no less.

Trained head to head against a control with the identical recipe and only the
encoder changed, the perfect-recall agent won 55.0% of games, interval 51.9 to
58.1. Against the reigning champion, 59.3%. And the win came with a visible change
in style: the new agent takes more goods than its predecessor, which is what
racing looks like. It is not just scoring its own hand anymore; it can see what the
opponent is collecting, and it has started denying.

## The trick that should have worked

Here is where the story turns. Because there is a much cheaper way to hand an agent
knowledge of hidden cards, and I tried it first.

Since the codec work, the strongest agent in the stable wraps the network in
search. The search deals with hidden zones the only way it can: it deals out a
guess. Before thinking, it fills in the opponent's hand and the deck with a random
arrangement consistent with everything public, thinks in that imagined world, then
repeats with fresh guesses and averages. Each guess is called a determinization,
and the averaging over many of them is what keeps the search honest about what it
does not know.

Which suggests an obvious shortcut. In a self-play harness I control both seats, so
I can skip the guessing and pin the *true* opponent hand into every imagined world.
No training, no new encoder: just stop lying to the search about the one zone that
matters most. Strictly more accurate information at zero cost. It has to help.

It lost five and a half points.

<ZoomImage src="/images/posts/ml4-pinned.png" alt="Two-panel bar chart. Left: search agents given the true deck or true opponent hand score below the genuinely blind agent (46.5% and 48.9% vs 52.1%). Right: the same knowledge trained into the encoder beats a matched control 55.0%." caption="The same knowledge, delivered two ways. Injected into the search's imagined worlds, it subtracts; learned by the network, it adds." />

The genuinely blind searcher won 52.1% of its games against the heuristic. Handed
the true opponent hand, the same searcher won 46.5%. Handed the true deck order
instead, 48.9%. Every flavor of truth made it worse, and the opponent's hand, the
most valuable-looking secret at the table, made it worst of all.

The mechanism took a while to believe. The strength of a determinizing search
*lives in the variety of its guesses*. Averaging over many imagined worlds forces
the agent toward moves that are good against the whole cloud of hands the opponent
might hold, which is another way of saying it hedges. Pin one world as the truth
and the hedge collapses: the search happily commits to lines that exploit that
exact hand, played by a network that never learned what to do with such
certainty. The averaging was not a workaround for missing information. It was
load-bearing.

So the same knowledge, delivered two ways, is worth minus five points or plus five
points. Injected into the search's imagination, it breaks the machinery. Baked
into the network's observation, where thousands of training games can shape what
to *do* with it, it is the biggest single gain since the pointer head. That night's
note in the journal reads: value must be trained in, not pinned. It is the closest
thing to a moral this project has.

## Nothing left to imitate

One experiment from the audit night completes the picture. Back when the clone
work localized the whole gap to card targeting, the natural follow-up was to stop
imitating and let reinforcement learning improve the take decision directly:
freeze everything else, warm-start from the imitation head, and train the take
choice against the heuristic with a win-or-lose reward.

Forty iterations of REINFORCE oscillated around parity and never left: best
checkpoint 50.5%, interval 44.8 to 56.3, straddling fifty. The heuristic's take
rule, one line of arithmetic trading token value against held cards, appears to be
close to optimal. There was no headroom to learn, because there was nothing left
to imitate or improve *in that decision*.

That flat line and this post's two wins are the same finding, seen from opposite
sides. Where the agent already saw everything that mattered, learning bought
nothing at all. Where its view was poor, fixing the view bought ten points, and
fixing its memory bought five more. Every point this project has earned since the
yardstick arrived has come from the interface: how the agent speaks, and now how
it sees.

So here is where things stand. The network sees every number a human at the table
sees. It remembers what it watched, forgets what it could not honestly know, and
names its moves properly. It races, it denies, it is measurably better than every
version before it.

And against a few hundred lines of if-statements, the ladder still reads level.
Seeing was worth points. Speaking was worth points. Whatever is left is not about
either.
